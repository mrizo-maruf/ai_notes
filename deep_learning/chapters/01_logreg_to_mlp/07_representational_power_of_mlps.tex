\section{Representational Power of MLPs}
\label{sec:logreg_to_mlp:representational_power_of_mlps}

\subsection{Separate three questions that are often mixed together}
\emph{Representation} asks whether some parameters can express or approximate a target function. \emph{Optimization} asks whether a training procedure finds suitable parameters. \emph{Generalization} asks how the resulting function performs on unseen data. A theorem about the first question does not automatically answer the other two.

A sigmoid or threshold perceptron without hidden features uses one affine score. Its binary decision boundary is a hyperplane in the given feature space. XOR fails the inequalities derived in Section 1.4, regardless of how long the perceptron is trained. An identity-output model without hidden layers is affine regression. A softmax linear classifier has pairwise boundaries $z_i=z_j$, hence affine boundaries; the same distinction between logits and probabilities applies.

The limitation depends on the representation supplied to the model. A linear classifier on manually constructed nonlinear features can have a nonlinear boundary in the raw inputs. Thus ``only linearly separable functions'' means separability in the model's input feature coordinates, not in every possible re-encoding.

\subsection{Any Boolean function on a finite cube: an explicit construction}
Let $f:\{0,1\}^d\to\{0,1\}$ be an arbitrary truth table. For each binary vector $v$ whose label is one, define the number of matching bits
\[
 s_v(x)=\sum_{i:v_i=1}x_i+\sum_{i:v_i=0}(1-x_i).
\]
This is affine in $x$. On binary inputs it equals $d$ if $x=v$ and is at most $d-1$ otherwise. Therefore one ReLU unit
\[
 h_v(x)=\max(0,s_v(x)-d+1)
\]
outputs exactly one at $v$ and zero at all other Boolean inputs. Set
\[
 F(x)=\sum_{v:f(v)=1}h_v(x).
\]
At most one such unit is active on each binary input, so $F(x)=f(x)$ there. This is a one-hidden-layer ReLU network with a linear output and at most $2^d$ hidden units. The all-zero truth table uses a zero output.

For XOR, this construction gives $h_{10}(x)=r(x_1-x_2)$ and $h_{01}(x)=r(x_2-x_1)$, exactly the two features used earlier. The construction is a finite-domain argument, not a claim that a continuous ReLU network equals a discontinuous Boolean indicator everywhere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. With a finite sigmoid output, probabilities cannot equal zero and one exactly, though thresholded labels can be exact and probabilities can approach those endpoints.

This proves existence by constructing weights from the entire truth table. It is not a learning procedure that discovers an unseen truth table from a small subset of its rows. The worst-case width is exponential in $d$, so even existence may be computationally impractical.

\subsection{Universal approximation: a precise statement}
A classical form of the result of \citet{cybenko1989} is: for a continuous sigmoidal activation $\sigma$, every continuous $f:[0,1]^d\to\mathbb{R}$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ admit a finite width $m$ and real parameters such that
\begin{equation}
 F(x)=c+\sum_{j=1}^m v_j\sigma(a_j^{\mathsf T}x+b_j),\qquad
 \sup_{x\in[0,1]^d}|F(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon.
\end{equation}
The output is a linear combination, not restricted to $[0,1]$. Other versions cover other suitable activations and domains. ReLU also has universal approximation results; the constructive one-dimensional proof below needs no appeal to the sigmoid theorem.

Read the quantifiers in order: choose a target and an error tolerance; then a sufficiently large \emph{finite} network exists. The required width and weight magnitudes can depend on both. A single fixed-width architecture is not promised arbitrary precision. No infinite network is required for a fixed positive tolerance, although a sequence of increasingly accurate approximations may require unbounded width.

Compactness matters. A continuous function on a compact interval or cube is bounded and uniformly continuous. Merely saying ``any bounded continuous function'' without specifying the approximation domain and norm is too broad. Uniform approximation on an entire noncompact space is a different assertion. Also, a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so continuous networks cannot uniformly approximate a jump discontinuity to arbitrarily small error across the jump. Weaker integral-error notions can permit approximating discontinuous targets.

\subsection{A first-principles proof in one dimension using ReLU hinges}
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, uniform continuity supplies $\delta>0$ such that $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $|x-y|<\delta$. Choose a partition
$a=t_0<\cdots<t_m=b$ with all intervals shorter than $\delta$.

On $[t_i,t_{i+1}]$, let $\lambda=(x-t_i)/(t_{i+1}-t_i)$ and define the linear interpolant
\[
 p(x)=(1-\lambda)f(t_i)+\lambda f(t_{i+1}),\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq1.
\]
Then
\begin{align*}
 |p(x)-f(x)|
 &\leq(1-\lambda)|f(t_i)-f(x)|+\lambda|f(t_{i+1})-f(x)|\\
 &<(1-\lambda)\varepsilon+\lambda\varepsilon=\varepsilon.
\end{align*}
At knots the error is zero. The interpolant is continuous and piecewise linear, so the hinge formula \eqref{eq:act678-hinges} represents it exactly. Two additional ReLUs can encode its initial affine slope if needed. Thus a finite one-hidden-layer ReLU network uniformly approximates $f$ within $\varepsilon$ on this interval.

This is a complete proof for the stated one-dimensional case. It does not constitute a proof of Cybenko's multidimensional theorem. The sigmoid-bump visualization provides another intuition, while the general result requires additional approximation arguments. ``Similar to a Taylor expansion'' is only a loose analogy: a Taylor series uses derivatives and powers around a point, may fail to converge to a smooth function, and does not explain approximation of every continuous function. Learned affine-transformed activations are a different function family.

For a twice differentiable $f$ with $|f''|\leq M$, linear interpolation on an interval of length $\Delta$ satisfies
\[
 |f(x)-p(x)|\leq\frac{M\Delta^2}{8}.
\]
The interpolation remainder is $f''(\xi)(x-t_i)(x-t_{i+1})/2$, whose absolute product is at most $\Delta^2/4$. For $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$, $M=2$ and uniform spacing $\Delta=1/m$ gives maximum error $1/(4m^2)$, attained at midpoints. This quantitatively explains the refinement figure in Section 1.6; width buys finer slope changes.

\subsection{What universal approximation does not say}
It does not specify a small sufficient width for every target, a practical sample size, a convergent optimizer, or good behavior outside the chosen domain. It does not remove noise, ambiguity in the inputs, or finite-precision limitations. It does not say that every parameter setting of a large network is useful.

The real Iris regression example is a concrete reminder. A nonlinear model can represent more functions, but its fixed training run had larger test RMSE than a straight line. A theorem that some wide network exists neither selects a good model for these data nor ranks the particular trained models. Fitting a finite training set and recovering its unknown data-generating rule are different achievements.

\subsection{Depth: repeated composition can reuse a small computation}
Consider the tent-shaped map on $[0,1]$,
\[
 T(x)=2r(x)-4r(x-\tfrac12)+2r(x-1)
 =\begin{cases}2x&0\leq x\leq\tfrac12,\\2-2x&\tfrac12\leq x\leq1.\end{cases}
\]
It maps both half-intervals onto the whole interval $[0,1]$, one in forward order and one in reverse order. Applying $T$ again performs the same fold on each half. Consequently $T\circ T$ has four affine pieces, and $T^{\circ L}$ has $2^L$ affine pieces. Their slopes have magnitude $2^L$, alternating signs.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{activations_1_6_1_7/depth_folding.pdf}
\caption{One small tent computation reused through composition. Each additional composition splits every current affine interval into two. These continuous triangular waves are exact ReLU constructions; a square-wave cartoon should not be read as an exact discontinuous ReLU output.}
\end{figure}

To see this is an actual deep network, write $v=(2,-4,2)^{\mathsf T}$ and $b=(0,-1/2,-1)^{\mathsf T}$. Let
\[
 h^{(1)}=r(\mathbf1_3x+b),\qquad
 h^{(\ell)}=r(\mathbf1_3v^{\mathsf T}h^{(\ell-1)}+b),\qquad
 F(x)=v^{\mathsf T}h^{(L)}.
\]
Every hidden layer has width three, and $F=T^{\circ L}$. The linear combination implementing one tent is absorbed into the next layer's affine map. The number of parameters is $O(L)$, while the number of affine pieces is exponential in $L$.

By contrast, a one-hidden-layer, one-input ReLU network with $m$ hidden units has at most $m$ distinct knots and therefore at most $m+1$ affine pieces. Each unit contributes at most one knot, and their sum cannot change slope elsewhere. To represent this same tent-composition function \emph{exactly}, it needs at least $2^L-1$ hidden units. This is a constructive representation-efficiency separation for a particular family, not a claim that every function benefits exponentially from depth or that approximation errors automatically obey the same exact-representation bound.

\subsection{Interpreting the linear-region theorem in the slide}
A linear region is a maximal connected input region where a piecewise-affine network agrees with one affine map. It is not a class label, an independent training example, or a complete measure of learning capacity.

For input dimension $n_0$, $L$ ReLU hidden layers, and widths $n_i\geq n_0$, \citet{montufar2014} give the constructive lower bound
\[
 R_{\max}\geq
 \left(\prod_{i=1}^{L-1}\left\lfloor\frac{n_i}{n_0}\right\rfloor^{n_0}\right)
 \sum_{j=0}^{n_0}\binom{n_L}{j}.
\]
For equal width $n$ divisible by fixed $n_0$ and $n>n_0$, this displays exponential growth in $L$. The slide summarizes the growth as
\[
 \Omega\!\left((n/n_0)^{(L-1)n_0}n^{n_0}\right).
\]
The floored expression states the explicit construction; the shorthand should be interpreted with its asymptotic regime. $\Omega$ is a lower bound on achievable maximal region count, not an equality for every network. Width and input dimension assumptions matter; for example the displayed base gives no exponential factor at $n=n_0$. The output map must preserve distinctions to retain all these regions.

Depth enables a later computation to act on many portions of the original input after earlier layers map them to common coordinates. Regions are not independent arbitrary pieces: they share parameters through composition. A network with all-zero weights remains constant regardless of depth. More potential regions therefore do not prove better accuracy, successful optimization, or immunity to overfitting.

\subsection{Capacity comparison and answers to the lecture questions}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm}p{5.1cm}p{6cm}}
\toprule
Architecture & What it can express & What remains unguaranteed\\
\midrule
No hidden layer & Affine regression; a sigmoid of an affine score; affine pairwise softmax boundaries & XOR in raw binary coordinates; arbitrary nonlinear dependence\\
One suitable nonlinear hidden layer & Arbitrary Boolean tables on their finite domain; arbitrary-precision continuous approximation on compact domains when width can grow & Small width, feasible training, accurate unseen predictions\\
Many nonlinear hidden layers & Compositions and reuse of learned features; efficient representations of some functions requiring many shallow units & An exponential gain for every target, every weight setting, or every data distribution\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The universal approximation theorem states an existence result with an error tolerance, a domain, and a suitable activation. It does not state a learning or generalization guarantee. A shallow universal family and a deep efficient family are not contradictory: both may approximate a function, while requiring very different resources.

\paragraph{Reconstruct the argument yourself.}
For a one-input ReLU network, count possible knots before counting parameters. For a tent composition, count how many old intervals are mapped across the next fold. For a Boolean lookup network, count matching bits. These three different counting arguments explain shallow shape flexibility, compositional efficiency, and finite-domain universality without relying on a slogan.
